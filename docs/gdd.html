01 // High concept

Ashfall is a compact, turn-based post-apocalyptic settlement strategy game about leading a fragile community from a temporary camp toward a lasting future.

The player governs through a settlement view, regional route map, short reports, and consequential events. Each turn balances immediate survival against long-term development. Growth is desirable, but every new resident, building, expedition, and alliance creates new needs and risks.

A completed run leaves behind a concise chronicle of who survived, what the settlement became, and what the player sacrificed to secure its future.

02 // Design pillars

Survival before expansion

Water, food, shelter, and safety are never completely solved. Expansion increases both resilience and demand.

Few, layered decisions

Depth comes from timing, trade-offs, and delayed effects—not constant micromanagement.

People, not counters

Deaths, arrivals, injuries, specialists, dissent, and departures remain visible and meaningful.

The region matters

Scouting, scavenging, trade, diplomacy, and threats connect city building to the wider map.

Growth changes the game

Scale creates options, but also factions, maintenance, attention, and political responsibility.

Every run is a history

Procedural sites, crises, and legacy choices produce stories without erasing careful planning.

03 // Core development loop

  1. AssessRead production, consumption, shortages, health, cohesion, threats, and messages.
  2. PlanAssign people, choose construction, set policy, and prepare a regional action.
  3. CommitSpend limited orders and supplies; reserve workers or expose them to risk.
  4. ResolveProcess production, expeditions, events, migration, injuries, and hostile actions.
  5. AdaptRespond as the settlement, region, shortages, factions, and opportunities change.
Read pressures → allocate people and supplies → build or reach outward → resolve consequences → grow into new pressures

One normal turn

  • One major settlement project or upgrade
  • One regional action: scout, scavenge, trade, negotiate, or attack
  • Workforce reassignment
  • One policy or event decision
  • Advance time

The order limit prevents the player from optimizing every problem simultaneously.

Three connected play spaces

Settlement

Build, repair, assign workers, manage capacity, and watch the community change.

Region

Discover sites and factions, establish routes and outposts, gather supplies, and answer threats.

Council

Handle petitions, policy, disputes, alliances, leadership, and survival’s social consequences.

These are not separate mini-games. A discovered water plant may require a workshop to restore and diplomacy—or force—to retain.

04 // Resource model

The interface distinguishes stockpiles, people, capacities, and conditions. Not every system becomes another spendable currency.

Water

Survival, farming, and some industry. Sourced from wells, collectors, purification, regional sites, and trade.

Food

Survival and health. Produced through gathering, farming, livestock, hunting, and trade.

Scrap

Construction, repair, tools, and trade. Recovered from ruins and scrapyards or gained through dismantling and tribute.

Fuel

Long expeditions, generators, vehicles, and advanced projects. Powerful, scarce, and maintenance-intensive.

People

Population is both the community and the workforce. Residents may be dependents, workers, specialists, injured, away on expeditions, or serving in the militia. Assigning people to one role removes them from another; risking them has human and economic consequences.

Capacities

Shelter, storage, production, medical care, workshops, defense, expedition range, and power come from buildings, equipment, and controlled sites. A full granary cannot solve inadequate food production or spoilage.

Settlement conditions

ConditionMeaningTypical effects
HealthNutrition, disease, injury, sanitation, and careLabor, mortality, recovery, population growth
CohesionTrust, morale, legitimacy, and cooperationProductivity, choices, desertion, unrest, faction conflict
ThreatExposure to specific hostile forcesRaids, demands, sabotage, escalation

Knowledge

Knowledge is unlock progress rather than an everyday stockpile. Specialists, archives, faction exchange, and projects advance thresholds that unlock small technology choices.

05 // Growth and progression

Advancement requires both population and a defining project. Population alone cannot rush development.

  1. I. Camp

    Gathering, tents, water collection, and short scavenging trips.

    Pressure: exposure, hunger, illness, no reserve.

  2. II. Settlement

    Farming, workshop, clinic, storage, basic militia, and migration.

    Pressure: maintenance, sanitation, consumption, raids.

  3. III. Township

    Specialists, improved workshops, local markets, and basic outposts.

    Pressure: competing interests, maintenance, nearby rivals.

  4. IV. City

    Advanced industry, districts, trade routes, council factions, and strong defenses.

    Pressure: inequality, infrastructure dependence, diplomacy, coordinated enemies.

  5. V. The Capitol

    Major infrastructure, regional governance, and a completed legacy project.

    Pressure: final crises, alliance obligations, debate over the future.

A City is achieved through population, infrastructure, and specialization. Becoming The Capitol is political: the City earns regional legitimacy through alliances, influence, controlled routes, and its legacy.

Population growth

Early growth comes primarily through migration and rescued survivors. Spare shelter, stable supplies, health, cohesion, reputation, relationships, and policy attract newcomers. Overcrowding creates sickness, ration pressure, and dissent instead of meeting an arbitrary hard cap.

Development branches

Sustenance

Water security, farming, storage, medicine.

Industry

Repair, construction, power, vehicles, scrap recovery.

Security

Scouting, fortification, militia, intelligence.

Society

Cohesion, education, diplomacy, migration, governance.

Anti-snowball principle

Prosperity creates responsibility. More people consume more; infrastructure attracts attention; specialists expect conditions; outposts need supply; alliances create obligations; power increases dependence on fuel and maintenance. Success stays strategically interesting without feeling punitive.

06 // Campaign goal

The recommended campaign combines a finite survival horizon with a chosen legacy project. The community must endure worsening regional conditions and commit to a durable future.

Green Haven

Restore regional water and build a self-sustaining agricultural society.

Focus: sustenance, health, cooperation.

The Network

Unite settlements through trade, radio, and mutual defense.

Focus: diplomacy, routes, reputation, cohesion.

Iron Refuge

Build a defended, powered stronghold capable of surviving alone.

Focus: industry, fuel, scrap, security.

Each path changes the final crises, allies, enemies, and epilogue. None is the universally “good” ending.

Victory and failure

Victory

  • Survive the final regional crisis
  • Complete one legacy project
  • Retain a viable population
  • Avoid total social collapse

Failure

  • No viable population remains
  • The settlement is destroyed or abandoned
  • Cohesion collapses irreversibly
  • The deadline arrives without a viable legacy

Severe setbacks should usually remain recoverable. A shortage, lost expedition, or raid creates a difficult chapter—not an automatic ending.

Campaign length

Prototype target: 40 turns across four chapters—establish the camp; secure production and explore; manage factions and regional threats; then complete the legacy and face the final crisis. The time represented by a turn remains a playtesting decision.

07 // Initial playable scope

Include

  • One settlement and a small route map
  • Four stockpiles and population assignment
  • Five to eight buildings
  • Scouting and scavenging
  • One neutral faction and one threat
  • A small event deck
  • Two settlement stages
  • A 12–16 turn scenario

Delay

  • Tactical battle maps
  • Detailed individual simulation
  • Multiple controlled settlements
  • Large technology trees
  • Extensive diplomacy
  • Multiple vehicle types
  • Endless mode

08 // Questions still open

  1. Is Ashfall the final title or a visual-concept codename?
  2. Is the central fantasy permanent rebuilding, or can migration remain a possible outcome?
  3. Should the tone favor hopeful reconstruction, bleak survival, or a balance?
  4. Should conflict remain strategic or eventually use a compact tactical battle screen?
  5. Is the regional catastrophe known, gradually discovered, or different every campaign?

09 // Current recommendation

Build around Water, Food, Scrap, Fuel, and People. Track Health, Cohesion, and visible threats. Advance through Camp → Settlement → Township → City → The Capitol.

Use a 40-turn finite campaign in which the player survives escalating pressure and completes one of several legacy projects. Ashfall’s purpose is not simply making numbers rise: the player decides what kind of society deserves to survive.
